# Literature review: is there real support for "composite/grafted" Voynich herbal plants?

Solo literature review by Claude, 2026-09-23. Follows the user's direction to
brainstorm broadly across research angles (not just the primary mechanism
thread); this is Historian-role context work, not a mechanism test, and
doesn't touch the coupling thread or any frozen script/manifest.

## The question

Voynich scholarship has long noted that most of the manuscript's ~130
herbal-page plant illustrations don't match any single known real species —
some combine a recognizable flower type with a stem, leaf, or root that
doesn't belong to that species. Is there real, citable support for the idea
that these are *deliberately or incidentally composite* — assembled from
parts of multiple real plants — as opposed to invented, stylized,
degraded-by-copying, or simply unidentified/extinct species?

## What's actually documented, and how credible it is

**A general historiographic pattern exists, independent of Voynich specifically**:
historians of medieval botanical illustration document a well-known
"copy-of-a-copy" degradation process — a scribe working from a damaged or
incomplete exemplar fills gaps by borrowing parts from a different model,
progressively losing the diagnostic features that would allow identification
across successive copies. This is a real, general phenomenon in the study
of herbal-manuscript transmission, not a claim invented to explain the
Voynich manuscript specifically.

**Two named, specific identification efforts exist for Voynich itself, and
both are significantly contested**:

- **Edith Sherwood** (independent researcher) proposed Mediterranean/Italian-
  origin identifications based substantially on color matching. This has
  been criticized directly and specifically — not just generically doubted —
  by Voynich researcher Nick Pelling: the color-based reasoning is
  "codicologically naive" because much of the manuscript's vivid coloring
  was very likely added later by different owners, not by the original
  artist, which undermines any identification built on matching painted
  colors to real species. Pelling also names a "botanical 'presentist'
  fallacy" — applying modern plant knowledge and modern species boundaries
  to a medieval illustration tradition that didn't share them — as a
  recurring failure mode in this kind of identification work generally.
- **Tucker & Talbert** (2013, *HerbalGram* 100, the American Botanical
  Council's journal — credentialed botanists, more cautious framing than
  Sherwood's) proposed 37 of 303 plant illustrations matching colonial-era
  New World (Nueva España/Mexican) flora, explicitly stating they weren't
  claiming proof, only that "more work ought to be done." Their accompanying
  claim of an underlying extinct Nahuatl-language layer was criticized much
  more sharply by a Nahuatl specialist as "largely bogus or heavily
  manipulated," and a linguist called the overall approach "pseudo-rigorous"
  — citations used for circumstantial support rather than genuine evidence.
  **René Zandbergen himself — whose ZL3b transcription this project uses as
  its canonical data source — was publicly skeptical of this hypothesis**,
  a directly relevant data point given how much this project already relies
  on Zandbergen's other work as authoritative.

**Neither source directly and rigorously establishes deliberate composite
construction.** Both are, at core, *whole-plant matching* attempts (does
illustration X resemble real species Y closely enough to identify it), not
studies that isolate and test which *specific parts* of an illustration
match which *specific* real species. The "composite plant" framing is more
an informal description commentators use for the *pattern of partial
matches* both efforts produced (a flower matching one family, a root or
leaf that doesn't) than a study that set out to test compositeness directly.
A minor, blog-comment-level mention of "synecdoche botany" (component parts
of several related plants) was found in discussion of Sherwood's work, but
it is not itself a developed or published theory — flagged here as an
unconfirmed lead, not cited as support, consistent with this project's
standard for search-summary-level claims.

## Honest assessment

This is a real, citable research angle with genuine scholarly attempts
behind it, but **both existing published attempts are significantly
criticized on methodological grounds that would need to be explicitly
avoided, not repeated**, if this project pursued it further:

1. Color-based reasoning is compromised by the codicological fact that much
   of the manuscript's coloring was likely added later, not by the original
   artist — any future attempt would need to work from line/form, not paint.
2. Whole-plant species-matching (rather than isolating and independently
   testing individual anatomical parts) has a documented history of
   confirmation bias in this specific manuscript's literature (the fish
   identification on f70v2 cited as an example).
3. Accompanying linguistic claims (as in Tucker & Talbert's Nahuatl
   hypothesis) have drawn sharp, specific criticism from a language
   specialist for using citations as circumstantial decoration rather than
   genuine support — a caution directly relevant to this project's own
   discipline about not treating a secondary source as more authoritative
   than it is.

## What this does and does not show

- It does not establish that Voynich plants are deliberately composite —
  that remains a real, unresolved, actively disputed question in the field.
- It does not identify the manuscript's origin, language, or plant sources.
- It does supply a citable historiographic anchor (copy-of-a-copy
  degradation as a real phenomenon in herbal-manuscript transmission) and a
  clear map of what's already been tried and why it drew criticism — useful
  before this project invests effort in its own identification attempt,
  since it names the specific pitfalls (color-based reasoning,
  whole-plant-only matching, over-citing circumstantial support) to avoid
  rather than rediscover.
- It is Historian-role context work, not translation-facing progress on its
  own — any future project-side attempt at composite-plant analysis would
  need its own scoping (what data, what method, what non-circular
  identification criteria) and is not started here.
